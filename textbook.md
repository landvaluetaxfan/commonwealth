# ONE — WHAT IS ACTUALLY SCARCE

The first thing a student of this economy must unlearn is the idea that things
are scarce.

They are not. Feedstock arrives from the belt in quantities no one has yet found
a use for. Fabrication is automatic, distributed, and so cheap that the accounting
cost of a manufactured object is dominated by the volume it will occupy once it
exists. If you want a chair, you may have a chair. The difficulty has never been
the chair.

What is scarce, in rough order of how much political time it consumes:

**Volume.** Pressurised cubic metres. Every one of them had to be built, sealed,
certified, and connected to an atmosphere plant, and the rate at which this can
be done is set by the construction schedule rather than by demand. You may have
any object you like and nowhere to put it. This is the fundamental good and the
defining domestic issue, precisely as land was in the agrarian economies of the
old world, and for the same reason: it cannot be manufactured at the point of
need.

**Thermal rejection.** This is the constraint that students find least intuitive
and that governs most. Energy is trivial — we are bathed in it. The difficulty is
the second half of the transaction. Every watt that does work becomes heat, and
heat in vacuum leaves only by radiation, and radiators are large, expensive,
fragile, and finite.

The consequence is worth stating plainly, because it is the single strangest fact
about our economy: **the number of minds that may exist is a public works
appropriation.** Not a market outcome. Not a natural limit. A line in a bill,
voted on by people you can write to.

**Substrate cycles.** Computation, which for a substantial fraction of the
population is not an input to life but the location of it.

**Delta-v and launch windows.** Movement between habitats is scheduled, rationed,
and cannot be increased by wanting it more.

**Bone-compatible bodies.** Bodies that tolerate one gravity. There are not many,
they cannot be made quickly, and the people who have them are worth a great deal.

The reader will notice that four of these five are not goods at all. They are
*permissions*: permission to occupy, permission to compute, permission to move,
permission to descend. An economy organised around permissions behaves very
differently from one organised around goods, and almost everything peculiar about
our politics follows from it.

# TWO — CLOSURE, AND WHAT HOLDS THE UNION TOGETHER

Every habitat has a closure ratio: the fraction of its material cycle it can
sustain without imports. A station at 0.95 recycles nearly everything and could,
if it chose, close its locks and continue. A station at 0.31 has fourteen days of
stored margin and then begins to die.

Ashfield Cans is at 0.31. Anselm Ring is at 0.79. L5 Refuge is at 0.91 and does
not let anyone forget it.

I want the student to sit with the implication rather than move past it. Most
federations are held together by force, by consent, or by a shared sense of
peoplehood. Ours is held together by **metabolic dependency**. A majority of our
member stations cannot leave, not because leaving is forbidden but because leaving
is fatal, and everybody involved knows this, and it colours every negotiation that
has ever taken place in this chamber.

From which follows the paradox that defines federal spending, and which I regard
as the central fact about the Commonwealth:

> Federal investment in a poor station raises its closure. Raising its closure
> increases its capacity to survive secession. **Every development appropriation
> is, on one reading, a subsidy toward the dissolution of the body making it.**

There is no way out of this. A government that refuses to develop the low band
holds the union together by keeping people poor, which is both monstrous and
unstable. A government that develops it is funding the thing that ends it. The
Chartists maintain that this is the whole of Commonwealth politics and that
everything else is decoration. They are tiresome about it and they are not
entirely wrong.

# THREE — RENT, AND THE COST OF CONTINUING TO EXIST

Ours is a rentier economy. Not in the loose sense the term is often abused into,
but literally: the dominant transaction in this economy is a recurring payment for
permission to keep existing in a particular place.

Consider what a household actually pays for.

**Volume rent**, on the pressurised space it occupies. **Thermal quota**, on the
heat it generates, whether metabolically or computationally. **Substrate rent**,
if any member of it runs as software. **Consumables**, at the metered rate above
the floor. Note that none of these is a purchase. There is no point at which any
of them is finished being paid.

This is why our tax base looks so strange to a visitor. We tax volume, thermal
quota, substrate-hours, and mass-to-orbit. We do not tax income, and periodic
proposals that we should are regarded as either naïve or foreign. The reason is
straightforward: income is not the binding fact about a person's economic
position. Outgoings are. A person's consumption of the finite is the thing worth
measuring, and it is also, conveniently, the thing that is already measured, since
every one of these is metered continuously by an authority that must meter it
anyway to keep everyone alive.

The Georgists observe that a volume lease derives its value almost entirely from
position — proximity to an agricultural deck, to a tether head, to the good air —
and that position is not created by the leaseholder. They are correct. Land value
taxation, an idea that spent three centuries as an interesting minor school on
Earth, turns out to be simply and obviously right in a habitat, where the total
quantity of the good is fixed by construction and its value is entirely
locational. The Georgists poll 4.1 per cent and have never exceeded it.

### The commanding heights

Air, water, thermal capacity, power, and substrate are natural monopolies with
captive customers and lethal failure modes. There is no meaningful sense in which
a household may decline to purchase atmosphere.

The whole economy is therefore, structurally, a regulated utility sector wearing
the clothing of a market. The argument about who should own the commanding
heights is not an argument between capitalism and its alternatives. It is an
argument about regulated monopoly, of exactly the kind that was settled and
re-settled a dozen times on Earth, conducted here at a volume appropriate to the
fact that a pricing error kills people.

# FOUR — TIME AS A CLASS SYSTEM

An emulated mind runs at a clock rate. The rate can be raised or lowered, and it
costs money, and this single fact reorganises the class structure of the
Commonwealth in a way that has no analogue anywhere in the historical record.

Consider two citizens.

The first runs hot, at eight times standard. She reads every bill. She attends
every hearing. She has, in the four objective years of a Parliament, lived
thirty-two subjective years, and she has spent a good deal of them working. She is
not more intelligent than her colleagues. She has simply had more time, and time
is a commodity she can buy.

The second runs slow, at three-tenths of standard, because the subsidy covers that
and nothing more. For her, the same Parliament lasts fourteen subjective months.
News reaches her compressed. A campaign happens around her faster than she can
follow it. She is not disenfranchised — no one has taken anything from her, and
her vote counts exactly as much as anyone's — but she cannot participate in a
conversation moving at twenty-six times her speed, and she knows it.

I ask students to notice how little of the ordinary vocabulary of inequality
applies here. She is not poor in goods; the floor covers her. She is not excluded
by law. She is excluded by **tempo**, and our language has no word for what has
been done to her.

The measurable quantity is subjective time experienced per objective year, and I
am aware of no other society in which this is a meaningful statistic. In ours it
is the most honest measure of class we have.

### The civic clock

From which arises a proposal that would be unintelligible in any other polity: a
**minimum civic clock rate**, publicly subsidised, on the argument that a
franchise exercised at a twentieth of the speed of the debate is not a franchise
in any sense the Charter intended.

It is expensive. It is thermally constrained, since a clock rate is a heat load.
And it is resented, sincerely and not unreasonably, by embodied workers who
observe that nobody has ever offered to subsidise *their* attention.

# FIVE — PERSONS, INSTANCES, AND THE SUPPLY OF LABOUR

*The reader should know that this chapter was written before the current bill and
will require revision.*

A running copy of a person is an instance. The law provides that an instance
separated from its root by less than the statutory divergence threshold — at
present one hundred and sixty-eight subjective hours — remains the same legal
person: no separate property, no separate liability, no separate wage, no separate
vote. Beyond the threshold it is a stranger with rights.

The economic consequence is enormous and almost entirely unremarked in public
debate.

An employer may spin an instance of a skilled worker, work it for a week, and
reabsorb it. The hours are performed. They are paid for once. And they are, in
every register that the Bureau counts, **invisible**: they appear in no
employment statistic, generate no separate wage, and confer no rights on the
entity that worked them.

My own estimate, and I stress that it is an estimate, is that instances perform
something close to **a fifth of all hours worked in the Commonwealth**, and hold
none of the jobs.

Lower the threshold to forty hours and roughly three hundred and eighty thousand
jobs come into existence overnight without a single person being hired. That is
what the current bill does. It is described in the chamber as a measure about
personhood, and it is, but it is also the largest single intervention in the
labour market that has ever been contemplated here, and I have not heard it
discussed in those terms once.

# SIX — WHO WORKS

Roughly 1.9 million people, out of some 4.9 million adults. A participation rate
near thirty-nine per cent.

| Sector | Share |
|---|---|
| Life support, maintenance, environment | 24% |
| Care | 19% |
| Verification, attestation, judgment under liability | 15% |
| Substrate, thermal, computation | 9% |
| Agriculture and consumables | 9% |
| Construction and volume | 7% |
| Transit and logistics | 7% |
| Government and administration | 6% |
| Culture, media, hospitality | 3% |
| Earth-facing gravity work | 1% |

A few observations for the student.

**Maintenance is the largest sector and will remain so.** Automation handles
regular geometry beautifully and awkward geometry not at all, and a two-hundred-
year-old habitat is nothing but awkward geometry: retrofits upon retrofits,
systems whose documentation vanished with the contractor that installed them, and
a failure mode that kills everyone in the compartment. We maintain preventively at
a cadence no commercial operation would tolerate, because we must. One worker per
fifteen residents. It is the ratio of a ship, and a habitat is a ship that never
docks.

**The third sector is our peculiarity.** Fifteen per cent of the workforce is
employed in verification, attestation, licensing, audit, underwriting, and legal
practice. This is very large. The reason is that automation can perform a task but
cannot *be liable* for it, and in an economy where an error is fatal, liability is
the scarce thing being purchased. Add to this that Schedule 2 of the Charter mixes
categories of substrate with categories of relation, so that every boundary case
between them is litigable, and reclassification has become a branch of practice
rather than an occasional matter. Our lawyers are numerous because our founding
document was drafted badly, and the drafting error has generated more employment
than any development programme in our history.

**Agriculture is larger than efficiency requires**, and I include this observation
knowing it will be objected to. A fully automated agricultural deck is entirely
achievable and marginally cheaper, and nobody wants one. The decks are where the
green is and where the light is long, and people court there and hold funerals
there, and a meaningful fraction of the nine per cent is doing that work because
they wish to. I regard this as the most encouraging statistic in the table.

**And one per cent do the gravity work.** Nineteen thousand people at the anchors,
paid extremely well, and almost all of them Earth-born, because they are the only
people whose bones tolerate the descent. The resentment this generates among the
orbital-born is not economic envy of the ordinary kind. It is the discovery that
the immigrant is not the cheap labourer of the old anxieties but the *privileged*
one, holding a qualification that cannot be earned, studied for, or acquired.

# SEVEN — WHO DOES NOT WORK

Three million adults are not in paid employment. I want to be careful here,
because the phrase invites a misreading.

This is not unemployment. There is no queue, no benefit office, no stigma of
consequence, and no political demand for job creation of the kind that dominated
the industrial economies. The consumables floor guarantees air, water, calories
and a minimum volume; substrate insurance guarantees continuation. These are not
a safety net beneath the economy. For a clear majority of adults, **they are the
economy**. The political argument is about the uprating formula, and has been for
sixty years, and is worth a great deal of money to a great many people, and will
never be resolved.

They are also not a bloc. This is the fact I most want students to retain, because
the Residual Constituency treats them as one, and the Residual Constituency
returns a single member.

| | Share |
|---|---|
| Post-career long-lived | 26% |
| Tempo-excluded | 19% |
| Unpaid carers | 16% |
| Students and the long-educated | 11% |
| Civic and cooperative labour | 10% |
| Fork-rentiers | 7% |
| Unattested and informal | 6% |
| Suspended | 2% |
| Unclassified | 4% |

**The post-career long-lived are the largest single group in the Commonwealth
and the reason very little changes.** People no longer die at the end of a
career; they accumulate. Someone who worked forty years and has been retired for
sixty is unremarkable. They are attested at very nearly universal rates. They vote
at very nearly universal rates. And they possess a resource that no working person
can match, which is unlimited time to attend the meeting, the hearing, the
selection contest, the party conference. Political scientists write about
gerontocracy as though it were a matter of attitude. It is a matter of
availability.

It is also why substrate insurance cannot be touched. To cut it is not to reduce
an income. It is to end a person.

**Fork-rentiers deserve more attention than they receive.** Two hundred and ten
thousand people are not employed, but their instances are: spun for a working
week, reabsorbed, and the fee paid to the root. They are, in the plainest terms,
renting themselves out.

Which means that two hundred and ten thousand people have a direct and substantial
financial interest in the divergence threshold remaining where it is. Lower it and
their instances become persons, with their own wages, their own substrate bills,
and their own votes — and the income stops.

I have watched the threshold debate closely and I have not once heard this
mentioned. The opposition to the bill is discussed entirely in terms of
restrictionism and wage competition. There is a large, cross-party, entirely
unrepresented economic interest sitting underneath it, and nobody has named it.

# EIGHT — THE FRANCHISE AS AN ECONOMIC INSTITUTION

Forty seats in the House of Delegates are returned not by places but by
occupations and industries. The student should understand these not as a curiosity
of our constitution but as an economic institution — arguably *the* economic
institution.

There are four kinds of functional franchise and they behave completely
differently.

**Licensure.** The electorate is the holders of a professional licence. Twenty-one
seats. And the licensing boards are appointed by the government of the day, which
means the government may widen or narrow an electorate **by regulation, with no
bill before Parliament, and no division**. It is not gerrymandering a district. It
is gerrymandering the electorate itself, and it is slow, deniable, and enormously
powerful.

**Corporate.** The company votes, not its employees. Eleven seats. Whoever
controls the company controls the seat. The Elevator and Loop Consortiums
constituency has **sixty-two** registered voters and returns four members.

**Union bloc.** The Maintenance and Trades panel casts for its members: two
hundred and fourteen thousand of them, seven seats. This is the reason the
functional tier is not uniformly aligned with property, and it is the reason the
Commons Union — which is pledged to abolish the tier — holds nine seats within it.

**Residual.** Everyone belonging to no recognised sector: the unemployed, the
dependent, the suspended. Three million nine hundred and ten thousand electors.
One seat.

Set them side by side, which is not customarily done:

| Electorate | Size | Seats |
|---|---|---|
| Licensed individuals | 25,190 | 21 |
| Companies | 613 | 11 |
| Union bloc | 214,000 | 7 |
| Everyone else | 3,910,000 | 1 |

Twenty-five thousand licensed persons, being one and three tenths per cent of the
workforce, return twenty-one members. Six hundred and thirteen companies return
eleven. It is not necessary to characterise this. The table characterises itself.

### Why nothing floats

Here I depart from the standard treatment, because the standard treatment does not
explain our capital markets and I believe this does.

We have no meaningful public equity market. Students often assume this is a
regulatory choice or a cultural accident. It is neither. It follows directly from
the corporate franchise.

If control of a company confers a **parliamentary vote**, then issuing shares
dilutes not merely a board but a seat in the House of Delegates. No consortium
holding a functional constituency will float, because floating is unilateral
disarmament. Ownership passes by negotiated transfer between families and
syndicates, the register of control is a political document rather than a
commercial one, and the great firms of the Commonwealth remain closely held two
centuries after they were founded.

Our capital markets are shaped by our electoral law. I know of nowhere else this
is true.

# NINE — MONEY, PRICES, AND THE THINGS A VOTE CAN SET

What we do have, in place of an equity market, is a market in permissions.

**Thermal quota** is traded, hedged, forwarded, and — with a regularity that the
Registry has stopped finding remarkable — defrauded. It is a market in permission
to exist at scale, and its price is set by an appropriation vote. When Parliament
funds radiators the quota price falls; when it does not, it rises; and the
difference is measured in whether a poor station can afford to keep its people
running.

**Substrate futures** are forward contracts on mind-hours: a traded market in
whether particular people continue.

**Substrate debt** is credit secured against one's own continuation, and I will
not editorialise except to say that the instrument is exactly what it sounds like.

**Underwriting** rather than banking is our dominant financial institution, for
the reason given in Chapter One: where failure is fatal, everything must be
priced continuously. The Underwriters are the only party in the chamber with
accurate figures on every question, which is why they are listened to and why they
are not liked.

### The unit of account

We denominate in thermal rejection capacity.

Students find this arbitrary until they attempt an alternative. Hours will not
serve, because clock rates differ twentyfold and a subjective hour is not a
constant. Mass will not serve, because mass is nearly free. Energy will not serve,
because energy is abundant.

Thermal capacity is the only quantity that is simultaneously objective, scarce,
and universally required. A unit of currency here is a claim on radiator area,
which is a claim on the room for something to happen, which is — if one follows it
all the way down — a claim on the room for someone to be alive.

I do not think our founders intended the metaphor. I think it is nonetheless the
truest thing about our money.

# TEN — SUSPENSION

A suspended mind is intact and not running. It is not dead. It is not alive in any
sense that permits it to do anything.

There are four routes in. **Voluntary**, waiting out a debt or a shortage of
bodies or a bad decade. **Penal**, a sentence served as absence. **Default**, the
simple cessation of ability to pay. And **triage**, in which a shortfall arrived
and the shed order made the choice.

The structural fact, and it is the cruellest in this book, is that **restoration
requires that somebody else pay**. The suspended cannot petition. They cannot
vote. They cannot be consulted about the terms on which they might be returned.
Their circumstances are decided entirely in rooms they are not in, by people under
no obligation to think about them, and the only thing that reliably moves those
people is an election.

Whether obligations continue to accrue during suspension is the question on which
everything else turns. At present they do, with the consequence that a person
suspended for default accumulates cost while being definitionally unable to earn,
and the arithmetic of their return worsens every day they are away. It is possible
to construct a person for whom restoration has become permanently unaffordable and
who has committed no offence beyond having been poor at the wrong moment. There
are, by the Bureau's count, some sixty-two thousand of them.

Two political facts follow.

The suspended are **counted for apportionment and cannot vote**. A station with a
large suspended cohort therefore returns members elected by a much smaller active
electorate, and those members represent, in the formal sense, people who have no
means of holding them to anything. Ashfield Cans has eleven thousand four hundred
suspended residents against fifteen seats.

And they are a bloc that can be suppressed **by choosing when to hold the
election**. I record this without comment. The practice has a name in the
literature and I decline to use it.

# ELEVEN — BODIES

Emulations may rent bodies. Bodies are scarce, expensive, and rationed by a
combination of price and waiting list.

This produces a class distinction *within* the emulated population that is
invisible from outside it: between those who are routinely embodied and those who
are permanently virtual. The former attend in person, shake hands, are seen. The
latter are not, and are treated accordingly, and there is no legal instrument
addressing it because no law has been broken.

Roughly forty-six per cent of jobs require an embodied worker. Maintenance in
awkward geometry does, unavoidably. Care almost entirely does. Attestation and
legal practice do not. The emulated poor are therefore structurally locked out of
nearly half the labour market by a constraint that is neither their fault nor
anyone's policy.

I want to correct a misapprehension that this chapter, read carelessly, might
encourage. **A body is a qualification, not a stigma.** Embodied work is not the
residue left to those who cannot afford to leave it; it spans the entire income
range, from the anchors — the best-paid work in the Commonwealth — to integrity
engineering, which is skilled, licensed, unionised, and in possession of a
withdrawal of labour that would kill everyone it serves. The embodied trades are
a labour aristocracy. Part of the reason the emulated poor are poor is precisely
that they have no body and cannot afford to rent one.

And beneath this lies the hardest fact in our society, which is not economic at
all.

A person born in three-tenths of a gravity, in a windowless can in the low band,
cannot descend to Earth. Not with difficulty. Not at cost. At all, and ever. Their
bones did not form for it and will not be persuaded. It is a permanent class
defined by skeletal density, it is transmitted at birth, it cannot be escaped by
any exercise of merit or fortune, and it is the reason the Descensionists are
tragic rather than merely absurd. They want to go home to a place they have never
been and cannot survive.

# TWELVE — THE BIOLOGICAL MAJORITY

A visitor reading the preceding chapters would conclude that this is a society of
emulated minds with some biological staff. It is worth correcting that impression
firmly, because the arithmetic runs the other way.

| Category | Share of adults |
|---|---|
| Biological | 64% |
| Emulation | 28% |
| Uplift | 4% |
| Synthetic | 4% |

Sixty-four per cent of us are exactly what our ancestors were: single-instanced,
embodied, running at one subjective second per objective second, and mortal.

The student is entitled to ask why, then, so much of our politics is about
substrate. The answer is not mysterious and has a great many historical
precedents. A minority which consumes a large and conspicuous share of a public
appropriation, whose entitlement cannot be reduced without ending its members,
and whose numbers are growing, will generate political conflict out of all
proportion to its size. Every society that has ever funded medicine from general
taxation has had this argument. We have it about radiators.

### The division of labour is not evenly distributed

Recall from Chapter Eleven that roughly forty-six per cent of jobs require an
embodied worker. Biologicals hold very nearly all of them.

The consequence is not stated often enough: **the working population is
disproportionately biological and the non-working population disproportionately
emulated.** Sixty-four per cent of adults are biological; a considerably higher
proportion of the 1.9 million in paid employment are. Maintenance in awkward
geometry, care, the agricultural decks, construction, the anchors — these are
performed by people with bodies, because they cannot be performed otherwise.

This single fact organises more of our class politics than any doctrine does.

### Four positions peculiar to the embodied

**They pay for substrate and do not use it.** Thermal taxation funds the
insurance that keeps emulated citizens running. From the perspective of an
embodied maintenance worker on Perigee Yards, the transfer runs one way: from
people who work with their hands toward people who do not have hands, some of
whom are simultaneously undercutting his wage by being forked at a hundred and
sixty-eight hours. I do not endorse this framing. I record that it is not
irrational, and that a party representing such workers which ignored it would not
represent them for long.

**They occupy volume.** An emulated household needs substrate and thermal quota.
A biological household needs pressurised cubic metres, which are the fundamental
scarce good of this economy and the one whose supply is hardest to increase. The
volume fight — density regulation, minimum-volume standards, the subletting
markets, the practice of partitioning a berth into six — is very nearly a
biological politics in its entirety. The Freeholders are a party of and about
embodied life, whatever their literature says.

**They die.** I have put this third but it is the most consequential thing in the
chapter, and I want the student to hold it steadily.

Biologicals die. Emulations accumulate. The gerontocracy described in Chapter
Seven — seven hundred and eighty thousand post-career long-lived, attested and
voting at near-universal rates, with unlimited time to attend every hearing and
every selection contest — is an *emulated* gerontocracy. Biological political
generations turn over at the old rate. The emulated ones do not turn over at all.

A biological citizen of thirty on Ashfield Cans is contesting the future of this
Commonwealth against an electorate that will still be voting in ninety years, and
that has already voted, consistently, for a settlement which serves it. There is
no constitutional remedy for this. There is not even a proposal for one that has
survived a first reading.

**And they cannot leave.** Bone density constrains the biological only. An
emulation of sufficient means rents a body certified for one gravity and descends
the well; the body is the emulation's equipment, not its self. An orbital-born
biological cannot descend, at any price, ever.

So the permanent class defined by skeletal density is specifically a *biological*
class, and the wealthy emulated may visit Earth while the poor embodied may not.
When Descensionists speak of exile they are speaking about the biological
orbital-born and nobody else, which is why the party's support sits where it does
and why its grievance is not answerable by economic policy.

### The question that actually sorts this society

It is not what a person is made of. It is **whether they can be switched off**.

A biological citizen on the consumables floor is poor. An emulated citizen on the
same floor is poor *and on the shed register*. The incomes are identical and the
exposures are not comparable: one of them can be ended by a radiator fault and an
appropriation vote, and the other cannot.

I set the four positions out, because I have never seen them tabulated and I
think the omission has done damage to the public argument.

| | Pays volume rent | Can be switched off |
|---|---|---|
| Embodied, employed | Yes | No |
| Embodied, on the floor | Yes, but cheaply | No |
| Emulated, high tier | No | Not in practice |
| Emulated, low tier | No | **Yes** |

The bottom row is the underclass of this Commonwealth. It is not a residue and it
is not an accident. It was produced by a schedule of prices, and the schedule was
set in a chamber by people who were elected.

### Uploading as an economic decision

Here I depart from the standard treatment again, because the standard treatment
discusses emulation as a philosophical event and I believe it is more often a
financial one.

Consider the balance of costs. Emulation is dramatically cheaper on volume and
dearer on thermal. So the household driven to upload is one that cannot make its
*volume* rent — which means, and this is the part consistently got wrong, that it
is a ring-band or middle-band household. Not a low-band one.

Volume on Anselm Ring is astronomical because everybody wishes to be there.
Volume on Ashfield Cans is nearly free because nobody does. Nobody uploads to
escape a rent they are already paying cheaply.

There is a physical reason the bands differ on the other side of the ledger, and
it is worth a paragraph. A habitat in low orbit attempts to reject heat while
sitting in the infrared glare of the Earth. The far band and the Lagrange
settlements have far better rejection geometry, and their substrate is genuinely
cheap. This is why Tsiolkovsky Deck is forty-seven per cent emulated and L5
Refuge fifty-one, while Ashfield Cans is eighty-one per cent biological. The two
scarcities run in opposite directions across the roster, and a household's
decision is made where they cross.

To upload on Ashfield would be to surrender cheap volume one already holds in
exchange for tier-four substrate on a station that has been shed for eleven
consecutive years. It is a death sentence with an additional step.

The pipeline, then, runs the other way from the popular account:

> Priced out of volume on a good station. Upload. Afford only low-tier substrate.
> Arrive on the shed register. Wait for a radiator fault.

Which is to say: **one may escape rent by ceasing to be embodied, and acquire in
exchange a mortality that is settled by appropriation vote.** It also removes
access to forty-six per cent of paid work, and returning to that work requires
renting a body at more than the volume rent one was fleeing.

The Bureau does not publish a figure for economically motivated uploading and has
declined three requests to construct one. My own reading of the Physiological
Medicine registers suggests it is not small, and that it is concentrated in the
ring and middle bands, precisely where volume prices have risen fastest.

### Two emulated populations, sharing nothing

It follows that there is no such thing as the emulated interest.

Wealth at the moment of uploading fixes substrate tier, and tier determines
whether a person accumulates for a century or is shed at the next shortfall. The
secure long-lived of Chapter Seven and the tier-four register of Chapter Ten are
the same legal category and have no common interest whatever. One of them wants
insurance protected; the other wants the shed order abolished; and a party
attempting to represent both will find that these are not the same demand.

I make no prediction about how the Public Substrate Association resolves this. I
observe only that it has not yet had to.

This is also, I think, the answer to a question students ask frequently about Root
and Vessel — how a party organised around continuity of the soul commands the
support it does in the low band. Its theology is not the whole of its appeal. Its
position amounts to saying that a person driven to upload by the volume market has
been done a wrong, and a great many people in the low band agree, on grounds that
have nothing whatever to do with the soul.

# THIRTEEN — WHAT THE COMMONWEALTH IS FOR

I have taught this course for a long time and I am asked the same question at the
end of it every year, generally by a student from the low band: what is this
union *for*?

The honest answer is that it is not for anything. It was not founded on a
principle. The Perigee Charter is a document of remarkable ambiguity, and its
ambiguity was deliberate, because its authors could not agree about the status of
emulated persons, or the limits of engineering authority, or whether sectoral
representation should be permanent, and they judged — correctly, I think — that a
document that settled these questions would not have been signed.

So they left holes, and we have spent two centuries arguing in them, and the
arguing is the constitution.

What holds us together is not principle. It is that most of our members would die
without the others, and everybody knows it, and this is a poor basis for a
nation and an entirely sufficient basis for a state.

I note in closing that we have never agreed on a word for ourselves.
*Circumterrestrials* appears in four founding documents and has never, so far as I
can establish, been said aloud by anyone who was not being paid to say it.
Proposals have been made and have failed. A people held together by shared
identity produces a name for itself without effort. We have not, because the thing
we share is not a thing anyone chose.

Ask a citizen of this Commonwealth what they are, and they will tell you the name
of a station.
